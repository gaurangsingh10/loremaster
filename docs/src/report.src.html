Loremaster

Canon & Chronicle: history, legend, and the line between them

Curiosity you can trust. Loremaster turns passive listening into active, sourced curiosity: stories gripping enough to steer, honest enough to show which parts are legend.

Loremaster is an AI storyteller for commuters: listener-steered episodes on history, science, politics and Star Wars / Warhammer 40K lore, with every claim graded as documented, debated, legend or fan theory. Twenty-one instant stories start in under a second; you can pause to ask the narrator a question, interview historical figures, and see the source behind any sentence.

2. Motivation

Who has this problem. People who spend 20–60 minutes a day commuting, walking or at the gym and love deep history or deep fictional lore. I'm one of them: I read Ken Follett and Joe Abercrombie, and I'd happily spend a train ride learning why the late Roman Empire looks so much like Warhammer's Imperium of Man. So are the SOM classmates who ride Metro-North and I-95, and the 40K and Star Wars fans I play and argue with.

How they deal with it today, and what it costs them.

  • Podcasts and lecture videos (Dan Carlin's Hardcore History, The Rest Is History, big-picture lecturers on YouTube, lore channels). They're brilliant, but the catalog is fixed. You can't say "go deeper on Diocletian, skip the battles," and you can't stop and ask "wait, is that actually true?"
  • Wikis and fan wikis are accurate-ish and sourced, but you can't read them while driving, and they don't tell a story.
  • ChatGPT voice mode will talk about anything. But it has no structure, forgets your thread tomorrow, and states legend and fact in the same confident voice. That's the real cost: you walk away "knowing" things that aren't so.

The myth problem, with my own example. I'd heard, and believed, that Texas community-property divorce law "comes from Muslim Spain." When Loremaster researched it, the sources (Wikipedia's articles on the Siete Partidas, the Visigothic Code and community property; UT Austin's Tarlton Law Library) point to Visigothic and Castilian law as the documented line into Spanish and Mexican Texas, with Islamic law at most a general influence on the Partidas. The fun version is a Debated claim at best. Popular history is full of claims like this, and nobody in the listening experience tells you which ones they are.

Why anyone should care. Popular history and lore are how most adults learn about the past. If the medium can't separate documented history from good stories, it quietly spreads myths. Loremaster keeps the fun and adds a one-tap answer to "is that true?"

3. What is new here

I looked hard at what already exists (searched October 23, 2026). Several products do pieces of this. None combines them, and none grades its own claims.

What Loremaster does that these don't:

  1. Every claim is graded, in two vocabularies. History gets Documented Debated Legend. Fiction gets Canon Legends Conflicting Fan theory, plus Unsupported for both. Star Wars literally has an official "Legends" label for the old Expanded Universe, and 40K contradicts itself on purpose; lore fans argue about exactly this.
  2. "The history behind the lore." Crossover episodes braid graded real history into graded canon (Roman Republic → Galactic Empire, late Rome → Imperium of Man, the military orders → Space Marines). I couldn't find anyone doing this; it ties together two audiences that already overlap.
  3. You steer a long-form story (10/20/40 min) with a choice at every chapter, and the Lore Library lets tomorrow's commute continue the thread.
  4. Interactive, not passive. Pause and ask the narrator anything (typed or spoken) and get a short, graded, sourced answer read aloud. Call it veils the next paragraphs until you predict what happened. Myth or Fact? ends each chapter with three quick calls and a streak. No podcast can do any of these.
  5. And more, built on the same graded core: science, technology and politics genres with their own grading rules; Snap & Story (photograph a statue or coin and a vision agent starts its story); Interview the past (Caesar answers in character, every claim graded); a story concierge; and a free on-device neural narrator, night/day themes and larger text.
Honest weakness: every individual piece exists somewhere. NotebookLM has interactivity; podcasts have better voices. Our novelty is the combination, plus claim-level grading. The local neural voice is good, but not a professional voice actor.

4. The agents

Eleven agents, all gpt-6-luna: the six in Figure 1 make each episode, and five more power the interactive layer (an Answerer for questions, a Quizmaster for games, an Identifier for photos, an Interviewer for in-character chats and a Concierge for recommendations). One narrow job each, typed outputs (Pydantic) as the contracts between them.

Figure 1. Agent organization. The Showrunner (boss) makes the creative decisions; code makes the handoffs, enforces limits and runs every deterministic check.

How they're organized, and why. Lecture 10's pattern lets a boss agent call specialists as tools in its own loop. I kept the same division of labor but made code do the routing. Three reasons: (1) the boss's context doesn't balloon with every chapter's evidence; (2) nothing can loop; (3) cost is predictable (Section 5). The Showrunner still makes every creative decision. Code also does everything that shouldn't need judgment: assigning evidence ids, the hallucination tripwire, budget checks, word counts, and stripping URLs from narration.

Model choice. gpt-6-luna for every agent, at "low" reasoning effort. It's the course model, and at $0.10 / $0.50 per million input/output tokens a 20-minute episode costs about a cent. It is the only model on the course key; with evidence cards and the Skeptic it leaves just 5% of claims unbacked (Section 5), so a pricier model (which I did not test) wasn't needed. Text-to-speech is not available on the course key (I tested: 404), so narration uses Kokoro, a free open neural voice model that runs locally (Section 5). The browser's own voice is the last-resort fallback.

The three newest agents are cheap to run: a photo identification costs $0.0004, an interview answer $0.0007 and a concierge reply $0.0004 (measured).

Model costs only. Hosting is not included, and phones can't run the local voice today, so a phone app would need the cloud-voice column (or pre-rendered audio) for live episodes.

The surprise: the thinking is cheap and the voice is expensive, so the voice runs locally. Six agents researching, writing and fact-checking a 20-minute episode cost about 1 cent. A cloud natural voice (OpenAI TTS) would add an estimated ~27 cents, 27× the agents (the course key has no TTS, so this is an estimate from published per-minute pricing). So the narrator runs on Kokoro, an open neural voice model running offline on the listener's machine: $0 per episode, about 5.5× faster than real time on a laptop (a 3.4-minute chapter renders in 37 s). Audio is rendered paragraph by paragraph, so the first section is ready in about 2–5 s while the rest renders in the background. It is cached by content hash, so a replay is instant and free. The per-user numbers above therefore include the natural voice at no extra cost; the cloud-TTS column is only for comparison.

What I did to keep cost down, with measurements

1. Prompt caching (−42.9%, measured). Long, stable content goes first in every prompt (shared house rules, the narrator style guide, then the role prompt), with a fixed prompt_cache_key per agent. scripts/cache_test.py ran the same 3 episodes both ways. The no-cache control puts a random line at the top of every prompt and is only valid if the API reports 0 cached tokens, which it did. The result: $0.00564 vs $0.00989 per 10-min episode (130,729 input tokens with 88,560 cached, vs 143,337 with 0 cached; 44 vs 47 calls; same ~68 s per episode). Without caching, every call also pays the cache-write rate (1.25× input) on its whole prefix.

2. Code instead of model calls wherever judgment isn't needed: page cleaning, keyword retrieval, evidence ids, the hallucination tripwire, URL stripping. 3. Capped evidence: ≤ 8 cards × 400 characters per chapter, so the Storyteller and Skeptic see ~3–4K input tokens instead of 50–150K-character pages. 4. Cheap gates first: the Guardian's regex runs before any model call, and its model check returns a tiny "choice". 5. Low reasoning effort and a fixed pipeline, so no agent spends tokens deciding whom to call. 6. Hard caps ($0.05 per episode; per-run call, tool and token limits) so a bug can't burn money.

Did cost cutting make it worse? Evidence

Skeptic calls are about 28% of episode spend, and the rewrite they trigger is billed to the Storyteller, so together they are the biggest cost I could cut. I measured whether they earn their keep. scripts/accuracy_eval.py takes chapter 1 of each of the 4 starter threads and writes one draft from a fixed evidence set. It then applies three treatments: off (no Skeptic), lite (Skeptic grades, no rewrite) and full (grades + one rewrite, the app default). It compares them with a plain gpt-6-luna "podcast narrator" given no evidence and no rules, which is what you get from just asking a chatbot. An independent judge pass (a fresh Skeptic call that ignores what each claim cited) grades every claim against the same evidence.

VariantClaimsJudge: supported as factShown as fact but not backed└ overstated└ unsourcedCost / chapter
Full Skeptic (default)6577%3 (5%)12$0.00225

No Skeptic6271%18 (29%)162$0.00103
Plain LLM1105%105 (95%)4164$0.00105
What this says, honestly. (1) Yes, cutting cost hurts quality. Dropping the Skeptic (and with it the rewrite) saves ~55% of chapter cost in this eval ($0.00103 vs $0.00225) but lets 29% of claims reach the listener as fact without backing, versus 13% with Skeptic-lite and 5% with the full Skeptic and rewrite. So the default stays "full". (2) Grounding still matters most: the plain LLM is at 95%. (3) Many baseline claims are unsourced, not false (64 of 105). (4) This is the second run of the eval, after the expert panel made grading stricter: claims must be backed as stated, interpretation is graded "Interpretation", and thin sourcing is capped at Debated. The first run, under the looser rules, scored full / lite / off at 2% / 4% / 4%. Stricter grading exposed what the Skeptic was quietly fixing (both runs are in output/). (5) The judge is the same model family as the Skeptic, so it shares blind spots. [GAURANG: spot-check 10 judge verdicts against the linked sources and report agreement here]
A finding I didn't expect: the gateway was replaying answers. During the first eval, two variants came back in 0.2 seconds with identical token counts. The Portkey gateway caches whole responses: send an identical request and it replays the earlier answer, with copied usage numbers. That would have made every listener of the same thread hear the same episode, and it corrupted my cost measurements. The app now sends x-portkey-cache-force-refresh, and I re-ran the cache test and accuracy eval afterwards. A gateway cache could cut repeat-thread cost to near zero, but for a storytelling product I chose fresh episodes.

Honest notes. (1) With defenses off, the model alone resisted the poisoned page every time. Curiosity, measured

The app is built to measure curiosity, not just listening. It logs each interaction anonymously (output/interaction_log.json) and summarizes it under Settings → Safety & transparency: "Is that true?" taps per episode, questions asked the narrator, Myth or Fact? and Call it accuracy, minutes listened, branches chosen and whether a listener would come back tomorrow. Listeners can switch the log off. So far it holds only my own and my classmates' informal tries, too few to report as evidence, so I don't quote numbers from it here.

8. Limits and next steps

What doesn't work yet

  • Voice. The neural narrator needs a one-time ~340 MB model download (optional for graders; without it the app falls back to the computer's own voice). It renders at ~5.5× real time, so fresh live chapters still take a few seconds to start.
  • Evidence is wiki-deep, not archive-deep. Grades are checked against Wikipedia and fan-wiki snippets, not primary sources or rulebooks. Lexicanum (the best 40K wiki) blocks bots, so I used the Fandom wiki.
  • Myths are sometimes hedged instead of named. The narrator sometimes says "the evidence doesn't establish…" (graded Unsupported) instead of stating the myth plainly (graded Legend).
  • Local only. Not hosted yet. Accounts live in the app's own database: you can change your password while signed in, but there's no email verification or forgotten-password reset.
  • Waiting on live episodes. Instant stories have no wait, but a brand-new topic still takes ~30 s for chapter 1 and ~15–30 s after each choice.

With another month

  1. Phone app, offline: pre-generate tomorrow's chapters overnight, and pre-write the likely next branch while you listen so choices feel instant.
  2. More genres, universes and languages: economics, art history, Dune or Tolkien are each one rules file; Kokoro already speaks Spanish, French, Hindi and Japanese.
  3. Deeper evidence: a primary-source tier (Wikisource, Internet Archive) and a different-model judge.
  4. Hands-free: ask questions by wake word without pausing, and let an interview guest hand off into a full episode.
