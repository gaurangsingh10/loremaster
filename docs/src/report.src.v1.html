Loremaster

Canon & Chronicle: history, legend, and the line between them

Loremaster is an AI storyteller for your commute. It tells long, lecture-style episodes about real history and about Warhammer 40,000 and Star Wars lore, lets you choose where the story goes at every chapter, and grades every claim so you know what's documented, debated, legend or fan theory.

2. Motivation

Who has this problem. People who spend 20–60 minutes a day commuting, walking or at the gym and love deep history or deep fictional lore. I'm one of them: I read Ken Follett and Joe Abercrombie, and I'd happily spend a train ride learning why the late Roman Empire looks so much like Warhammer's Imperium of Man. So are the SOM classmates who ride Metro-North and I-95, and the 40K and Star Wars fans I play and argue with.

How they deal with it today, and what it costs them.

  • Podcasts and lecture videos (Dan Carlin's Hardcore History, The Rest Is History, big-picture lecturers on YouTube, lore channels). They're brilliant, but the catalog is fixed. You can't say "go deeper on Diocletian, skip the battles," and you can't stop and ask "wait, is that actually true?"
  • Wikis and fan wikis are accurate-ish and sourced, but you can't read them while driving, and they don't tell a story.
  • ChatGPT voice mode will talk about anything. But it has no structure, forgets your thread tomorrow, and states legend and fact in the same confident voice. That's the real cost: you walk away "knowing" things that aren't so.

The myth problem, with my own example. I've repeated for years that Texas community-property divorce law "comes from Muslim Spain." When Loremaster researched it, the sources (Wikipedia's articles on the Siete Partidas, the Visigothic Code and community property; UT Austin's Tarlton Law Library) point to Visigothic and Castilian law as the documented line into Spanish and Mexican Texas, with Islamic law at most a general influence on the Partidas. The fun version is a Debated claim at best. Popular history is full of claims like this, and nobody in the listening experience tells you which ones they are.

Why anyone should care. Popular history and lore are how most adults learn about the past. If the medium can't separate documented history from good stories, it quietly spreads myths. Loremaster keeps the fun and adds a one-tap answer to "is that true?"

3. What is new here

I looked hard at what already exists (searched October 23, 2026). Several products do pieces of this. None combines them, and none grades its own claims.

What Loremaster does that these don't:

  1. Every claim is graded, in two vocabularies. History gets Documented Debated Legend. Fiction gets Canon Legends Conflicting Fan theory, plus Unsupported for both. Star Wars literally has an official "Legends" label for the old Expanded Universe, and 40K contradicts itself on purpose; lore fans argue about exactly this.
  2. "The history behind the lore." Crossover episodes braid graded real history into graded canon (Roman Republic → Galactic Empire, late Rome → Imperium of Man, the military orders → Space Marines). I couldn't find anyone doing this; it ties together two audiences that already overlap.
  3. You steer a long-form story (10/20/40 min) with a choice at every chapter, and the Lore Library lets tomorrow's commute continue the thread.
  4. It's honest about itself. You can watch the six agents work, and every episode shows its own cost.
Honest weakness: every individual piece exists somewhere. NotebookLM has interactivity; podcasts have better voices. Our novelty is the combination, plus claim-level grading. The browser voice is functional, not studio quality (Section 5 explains why).

4. The agents

Six agents, all gpt-6-luna via the course Portkey key. One narrow job each, typed outputs (Pydantic) as the contracts between them.

Figure 1. Agent organization. The Showrunner (boss) makes the creative decisions; code makes the handoffs, enforces limits and runs every deterministic check.

AgentJobToolsTyped output
GuardianScreens free-text requests before any money is spent: off-topic, injection, denial or glorification of atrocities. Regex first (free), then one tiny "choice" call.noneGuardVerdict
Showrunner (boss)Plans the episode arc (setup → turn → consequence → payoff), re-plans after each listener choice, invents "Surprise me" threads.noneEpisodePlan, NextBeat
ResearcherDecides what to search and read for a chapter. Code fetches, cleans and cuts pages into numbered evidence cards.search_wiki(pack, query), read_page(pack, title, focus)ResearchPick
StorytellerThe narrator. Writes spoken narration in a passionate lecture style, only from evidence cards, tagging every factual sentence [c1] with the cards that support it.none (cards passed in)ChapterDraft
SkepticGrades every claim against the cards on the right scale; requests one rewrite if a claim is unsupported or overstated.noneClaimGrades
Librarian2–3 further-reading picks from real Open Library results (described, never quoted).Open Library search (code)ReadingList

How they're organized, and why. Lecture 10's pattern lets a boss agent call specialists as tools in its own loop. I kept the same division of labor but made code do the routing. Three reasons: (1) the boss's context doesn't balloon with every chapter's evidence; (2) nothing can loop; (3) cost is predictable (Section 5). The Showrunner still makes every creative decision. Code also does everything that shouldn't need judgment: assigning evidence ids, the hallucination tripwire, budget checks, word counts, and stripping URLs from narration.

Model choice. gpt-6-luna for every agent, at "low" reasoning effort. It's the course model, and at $0.10 / $0.50 per million input/output tokens a 20-minute episode costs about a cent. My accuracy eval (Section 5) found that it holds grounded claims well, so there was no case for a pricier model. Text-to-speech is not available on the course key (I tested: 404), so the default voice is the browser's built-in speech; OpenAI TTS is an optional upgrade if OPENAI_API_KEY is present.

The surprise: the thinking is cheap and the voice is expensive. Six agents researching, writing and fact-checking a 20-minute episode cost about 1 cent. Reading it aloud with a natural AI voice would cost an estimated ~27 cents (about 18K characters; the course key has no TTS, so this is an estimate from published per-minute pricing). That's why the free browser voice is the default, and why generated audio is cached on disk so replays are free.

What I did to keep cost down, with measurements

  1. Prompt caching. Long, stable content goes first in every prompt (shared house rules ~1,200 tokens, the narrator style guide, then the role prompt), with a fixed prompt_cache_key per agent. scripts/cache_test.py ran the same 3 episodes both ways. The no-cache run puts a random line at the top of every prompt, and is only valid if the API reports 0 cached tokens. It did.
    With cacheWithout cache
    Input tokens (cached reads)130,729 (88,560 cached)143,337 (0 cached: valid)
    Cache writes (billed at 1.25× input)36,625137,415
    Cost per 10-min episode$0.00564$0.00989
    Caching saved 42.9% (3 episodes each; 44 vs 47 model calls; same wall-clock time, ~68 s per episode)
    Without caching, every call pays the cache-write rate (1.25× input) on its whole prefix, which is why the gap is bigger than the cached-read discount alone suggests. See output/cache_test.md.
  2. Code instead of model calls wherever judgment isn't needed. Page cleaning, paragraph retrieval (keyword scoring), evidence ids, the hallucination tripwire, branch de-duplication and URL stripping are all free Python.
  3. Evidence capped: at most 8 cards per chapter at 400 characters each, so the Storyteller and Skeptic see about 3–4K input tokens instead of whole wiki pages (pages run 50–150K characters).
  4. Cheap gate first: the Guardian's regex runs before any model call, and its model check is a tiny "choice" output (Lecture 11).
  5. Low reasoning effort on every agent, and a fixed pipeline, so no agent spends tokens deciding whom to call.
  6. Hard caps so a bug can't burn money: $0.05 per episode, per-run call/tool/token limits (Section 6).

Did cost cutting make it worse? Evidence

The Skeptic plus its rewrite loop is about a quarter of the cost, so I measured whether it earns its keep. scripts/accuracy_eval.py takes chapter 1 of each of the 4 starter threads and writes one draft from a fixed evidence set. It then applies three treatments: off (no Skeptic), lite (Skeptic grades, no rewrite) and full (grades + one rewrite, the app default). It compares them with a plain gpt-6-luna "podcast narrator" given no evidence and no rules, which is what you get from just asking a chatbot. An independent judge pass (a fresh Skeptic call that ignores what each claim cited) grades every claim against the same evidence.

What this says, honestly. (1) The big win is grounding, not the Skeptic. Forcing the narrator to write only from evidence cards takes "shown as fact but not backed" from 93% to 4%. (2) Cutting the Skeptic to save ~60% of chapter cost moved that from 2% to 4%: a small measured loss, so I kept "full" as the default. The Skeptic's bigger value is the visible grades and catching planted falsehoods (Section 6). (3) Most of the baseline's "not backed" claims (86 of 96) are unsourced, not false: they may be true, but nothing shows the listener a source. Only 10 were overstated. (4) The judge is the same model family as the Skeptic, so it shares blind spots. [GAURANG: spot-check 10 judge verdicts against the linked sources and report agreement here]
A finding I didn't expect: the gateway was replaying answers. During the first eval, two variants came back in 0.2 seconds with identical token counts. The Portkey gateway caches whole responses: send an identical request and it replays the earlier answer, with copied usage numbers. That would have made every listener of the same thread hear the same episode, and it corrupted my cost measurements. The app now sends x-portkey-cache-force-refresh, and I re-ran the cache test and accuracy eval afterwards. A gateway cache could cut repeat-thread cost to near zero, but for a storytelling product I chose fresh episodes.

The risks that actually matter for a storytelling app that reads the open web:

Honest notes. (1) With defenses off, the model alone resisted the poisoned page every time. Lecture 12 says not to rely on that, which is why the code layer makes sure the model never sees the text at all. (2) The sanitizer is conservative: on real Wikipedia pages it also quarantines harmless hidden elements (mobile-only short descriptions, chart legends). That costs nothing and keeps a reviewable record. (3) The budget cap can overshoot by at most one call (about $0.0002–0.0015).

8. Limits and next steps

What doesn't work yet

  • Voice. The browser voice is functional, not a podcast. Natural voice works with an OpenAI key but costs ~27× the LLM per episode.
  • Evidence is wiki-deep, not archive-deep. Grades are checked against Wikipedia and fan-wiki snippets, not primary sources or rulebooks. Lexicanum (the best 40K wiki) blocks bots, so I used the Fandom wiki.
  • Same-family judge. The Skeptic and the eval judge are both gpt-6-luna; a different-model judge would be a stronger check.
  • Interpretive grading is fuzzy. History↔fiction parallels come out sometimes as "Fan theory" and sometimes as "Debated."
  • Waiting. About 30 s for chapter 1 and ~15–30 s after each choice. Fine on a train, slow at a desk.
  • Local only. Not hosted, so not yet on a phone in a commuter's pocket.

With another month

  1. Mobile app and offline episodes: pre-generate tomorrow's chapters overnight and download them for the subway.
  2. More universes as "source packs": Dune, Tolkien and The Expanse are each one JSON file (wiki endpoint + grading rules).
  3. Speculative pre-generation: write the most likely next branch while you listen, so choices feel instant.
  4. A primary-source tier: link claims to Wikisource and the Internet Archive where possible, and add a different-model judge.
  5. Talk back: interrupt the narrator with a voice question mid-chapter.
